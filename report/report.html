Predicting Airbnb Listing Price Across Different Cities

Dhanush S S (PES1UG24AM082)  ·  Harsha K R (PES1UG24AM114)
UE24CS352A Machine Learning, Mini Project 71 · Reference: Luo, Zhou & Zhou, Stanford CS229 (2019)

1. Problem statement

Airbnb hosts set their nightly price by guesswork, and guests cannot easily tell whether a price is fair. We predict the nightly price of a listing from its attributes (size, room type, location, amenities, reviews, host details and description) in three cities, New York City, Paris and Berlin, and ask two further questions: does one model trained on several cities work as well as separate models, and can a model trained in some cities price listings in a city it has never seen?

2. Dataset

We use the public Inside Airbnb detailed listings files (scraped June/July 2026; 90 columns per listing). Listings without a price were removed, as were prices outside 10 to 1000 USD per night (1.3% to 5% per city). Paris and Berlin prices (EUR) were converted to USD at a fixed 1.15. Each city was split 80/20 into train and test with a fixed seed, and every experiment is scored on the same test listings.

3. Approach

  • Target. Prices are strongly right-skewed, so all models predict log(price); as in the reference report this was the single most useful transformation.
  • Features (189 in total), in groups. C, continuous: accommodates, bedrooms, beds, bathrooms (parsed from text) and a shared-bath flag, minimum/maximum nights, availability, review counts and the seven review scores, host tenure and listing counts, superhost and licence flags, amenity count, description length, distance to the city centre and latitude/longitude; median-imputed with missing-value flags and standardised. O, categorical: room type and property type (one-hot) and the 60 most common amenities (multi-hot). T, text: TF-IDF of title and description on uni- and bi-grams, reduced to 50 dimensions by truncated SVD.
  • Leakage control. Columns derived from the price itself (price_quote_*, estimated_revenue_l365d) and the length of the stay that was quoted were excluded.
  • Models. Ridge regression and K-nearest neighbours (baselines), Random Forest, XGBoost (hyper-parameters chosen by 3-fold random search on the NYC training set, CV R² 0.848) and a neural network (MLP, 64 and 32 ReLU units, Adam, early stopping). Metrics: R² and MSE on log price, plus MAE and median percentage error in dollars.

4. Implementation

Python with pandas, scikit-learn and XGBoost. The whole feature pipeline is a single scikit-learn ColumnTransformer (custom transformers for amenities and text), so the same object is fitted on training data only and reused for prediction, which prevents test-set leakage. scripts/prepare_data.py cleans the raw files, scripts/train.py runs all four experiments and writes metrics and figures, and the final model (XGBoost on all three cities with a city indicator) is saved and served by a command-line predictor and a Streamlit web app for the demo. A Jupyter notebook walks through the analysis.

  • XGBoost is the best model in every city (test R² 0.850 NYC, 0.838 Paris, 0.895 Berlin; median error about 15% of the price), ahead of Random Forest and the MLP; the linear and KNN baselines trail by 0.03 to 0.19. The reference report reached 0.741 (NYC) and 0.704 (Paris).
  • Structured features carry the signal. Continuous features alone reach 0.84/0.82; categorical and amenity features add about 0.015, and the description text adds almost nothing on top because it mostly restates the structured fields. The strongest single features are the shared-bath flag, licence status, room type, guest capacity and minimum stay.
  • One model for two cities (NYC + Paris) is nearly as accurate as two separate models (0.836 vs 0.844 and 0.825 vs 0.829), so a single multi-city model is practical.
  • Transfer depends on market similarity, not data size. A Paris model prices unseen Berlin listings with R² 0.770, but a NYC model only 0.226, and adding NYC to Paris lowers Berlin accuracy to 0.694. Since NYC's 2023 short-term rental law, 78% of its listings require stays of 28+ nights, which are quoted at discounted monthly rates, so NYC prices follow different rules from the European cities. This differs from the reference report, where combining cities helped.

6. Conclusions

Gradient-boosted trees on log price, with carefully cleaned structured features, predict Airbnb prices well in all three cities (R² 0.84 to 0.90 on log price, typical error about 15%), clearly improving on linear, KNN and neural-network models and on the 2019 reference results. A single model can serve several cities, and a model from a similar market (Paris) generalises to a new city (Berlin) far better than one from a market with different regulation (NYC). Limits: prices are quoted rates at scrape time rather than booked prices, seasonality is not modelled, and a fixed exchange rate is used. Future work: add date and seasonality features, calendar data, and domain adaptation (e.g. per-city re-weighting) to improve transfer to new cities.
